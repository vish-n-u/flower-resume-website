How Recruiters Actually Read Your Resume: What the Research Says

Eye-tracking, hiring simulations, ATS data and 2026 hiring-manager surveys, with what each one does and doesn't prove, and what to change on your resume.

By the Flower Resume team · Published November 24, 2026 · 11 min read · 14 sources

Almost every piece of resume advice comes with a confident claim about recruiters: they only look for six seconds, robots reject 75% of resumes, never go over one page. Some of these come from real research. Some are distortions of it. A few appear to have no source at all.

We went back to the studies people usually cite (and a few newer ones from 2025 and 2026) to see what was actually measured, and what it means for how you should write your resume. Where a study has real limitations, we say so.

The short version

  • The first skim is fast — about 7 seconds in the best-known eye-tracking study — and it is mostly spent on job titles and structure.
  • Resumes that survive the skim get read much more carefully, sometimes for minutes.
  • Nearly every large employer uses an ATS, but it mostly works as a search engine. Recruiters filter by skills, titles, education and certifications.
  • Rigid filters do screen out qualified people. Mirroring the posting's real requirements in plain language is your best defense.
  • Hiring managers say they increasingly notice generic, AI-sounding resumes — and treat them as a red flag.

1. The first pass really is a skim — about 7 seconds

The "six-second resume" comes from Ladders, a U.S. job site, which ran an eye-tracking study of recruiters in 2012. It repeated the study in 2018 and found the average initial screen had risen to 7.4 seconds. Eye-tracking records exactly where a reader's gaze goes, so this is one of the few resume studies that measures behavior rather than opinions.

Two findings matter more than the headline number:

  • Job titles got the most attention. Recruiters spent more time on titles than on any other element.
  • Layout decided whether the skim worked. The resumes that performed best had simple layouts with clearly marked section and job headers. The worst had long sentences, multiple columns, little white space, and no headers to guide the eye down the page.

One caveat: the 2018 report does not say what kinds of roles were being screened or how long the resumes were, a point raised by recruiting publication ERE. Treat 7.4 seconds as "a quick first skim", not a precise law.

What to do with this

  • Put each job title on its own line, in bold, with the employer and dates next to it.
  • Use one column and standard section names (Experience, Education, Skills).
  • Start bullets with the result or the action, not with "Responsible for".
  • If your official title is unusual, add a plain-English version in brackets: "Associate II, Insights (Product Analytics)".

2. Pass the skim, and they read properly

The skim is only stage one. In a 2018 hiring simulation run by resume-writing service ResumeGo, 482 recruiters, hiring managers and HR professionals screened resumes for a range of roles. They spent an average of 2 minutes 24 seconds on one-page resumes and 4 minutes 5 seconds on two-page resumes.

That doesn't contradict Ladders. It shows two different stages: a fast filter, then a real evaluation of the shortlist. Your resume has to work for both. The structure gets you through the skim, and the substance (specific results, scope, evidence) gets you the interview.

The simulation is also why "two pages is fine" became popular advice. Real interview data tells a more complicated story, which we cover in How Long Should a Resume Be?

3. Nearly every big employer uses an ATS — but it's mostly a search box

Jobscan checks the career sites of every Fortune 500 company each year to see which applicant tracking system (ATS) they use. In 2025 it detected an ATS at 97.8% of them, and the figure has stayed around 97–99% since 2018. Workday is the most common, used by over 40% of the list.

The popular claim that "the ATS rejects 75% of resumes before a human sees them" doesn't have a clear source, and it misdescribes how these systems usually work. An ATS stores and organizes applications. Recruiters then search and filter that database, much like you'd filter products on a shopping site. In a 2025 Jobscan survey of 384 recruiters:

4. Rigid filters do screen out qualified people

The most rigorous evidence that screening systems miss good candidates comes from Harvard Business School and Accenture. Their 2021 report, Hidden Workers: Untapped Talent, surveyed 8,720 workers and 2,275 executives in the US, UK and Germany. It estimated that more than 27 million people in the US were "hidden" from employers by conventional recruiting systems.

Among employers surveyed, 88% said qualified high-skilled candidates were screened out because they didn't exactly match the hiring criteria, and 94% said the same of middle-skilled candidates. The report's authors note that systems often filter on what's missing, such as a degree or an unexplained gap, rather than on what a candidate can do.

The report's lead author argues these systems only match what they're configured to look for:

In that interview he gave the example of a resume ranked low because the system didn't treat living in five countries as "international experience". The candidate had the experience. The system just didn't recognize how it was worded.

What to do with this

  • Go through the "Requirements" section line by line. For each requirement you genuinely meet, make sure your resume says so in similar words.
  • If you have an equivalent instead of the exact requirement (e.g. 6 years' experience instead of a degree), state it plainly in your summary.
  • If you have a gap, give it one honest line (e.g. "Career break — family care, 2023–2024") rather than leaving a hole for a filter or a reader to guess about.

5. Matching the job title is one of the strongest signals

In its 2025 State of the Job Search report, built on more than 2.5 million job applications, Jobscan found that resumes whose job title matched the listing had an interview rate 10.6 times higher than those that didn't. Applications with a cover letter had a 3.4 times higher interview rate.

This is correlation, not proof. People whose titles match are often simply better-fit candidates. But it lines up with the Ladders finding that titles draw the most attention, and with recruiters searching by title. The lesson isn't to fake a title. It's to make sure the title you're targeting appears in your resume when it honestly describes your work, for example in your headline or summary ("Data analyst with 4 years in e-commerce…").

6. Recruiters say they can spot AI-written resumes

This is the newest finding, and it matters if you use AI tools, including ours. In Resume Genius's 2026 survey of 1,000 U.S. hiring managers, 80% said they can often tell when a resume was written by AI, 77% said many resumes they see look fully or partly AI-generated, and 69% said resumes have become more generic over the past five years.

The giveaways they named were unnatural phrasing (51%), repetitive or generic language (44%), and vague or inflated descriptions (41%). Note that these are things hiring managers believe they can detect. A survey can't tell us how accurate they are. Either way, generic writing hurts you, whoever wrote it. We wrote a full guide on using AI without sounding like AI.

A checklist based on the evidence

  1. Make titles, employers and dates scannable: one line each, consistent format.
  2. Use one column and standard headers. It helps both the 7-second skim and ATS parsing.
  3. Mirror the posting's must-have skills using its exact terms, wherever you genuinely have them.
  4. Put the target job title in your headline or summary if it truthfully describes you.
  5. Lead with your most relevant role and results, because the top third gets the most attention.
  6. Address equivalents and gaps in one plain line instead of hoping no one notices.
  7. Replace adjectives with evidence: numbers, scope, named tools, named outcomes.
  8. Tailor for each application. Every point above depends on the specific posting.

A note on the research itself

Most resume research is done by companies that sell resume services, including Ladders, ResumeGo, Jobscan and Resume Genius, and very little of it is peer-reviewed. The Harvard/Accenture report is the notable exception in rigor. We've used these studies because they're the best available, but treat the exact numbers as directional. When two sources disagree, as they do on resume length, we've said so.
