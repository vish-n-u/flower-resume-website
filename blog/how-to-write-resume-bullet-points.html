Resume Writing

How to Write Resume Bullet Points
That Get You Interviews

The simple formula for writing achievement-driven bullets that make recruiters stop scrolling — with real before/after examples.

July 2026 · 9 min read

Your resume bullet points are doing most of the heavy lifting. They're what recruiters actually read — everything else is just context. And yet most people write bullets that say almost nothing.

"Responsible for managing social media accounts." "Helped with customer service." "Worked on various projects."

These tell a recruiter nothing useful. They're job descriptions, not accomplishments. Here's how to fix them.

The Formula: Action + Task + Result

Every strong resume bullet follows this structure:

[Strong Action Verb] + [What You Did] + [Measurable Result]

Example: Increased email open rates by 34% by rewriting subject lines and segmenting the list by purchase history.

This formula works because it immediately answers the only question a recruiter has: "What did this person actually accomplish?"

Step 1 — Start With a Strong Action Verb

The first word of your bullet sets the entire tone. Weak openers like "Helped," "Assisted," "Worked on," or "Responsible for" bury your contribution. Start with a verb that owns the action.

Leadership

  • Led
  • Directed
  • Managed
  • Oversaw
  • Spearheaded

Growth

  • Grew
  • Increased
  • Scaled
  • Expanded
  • Boosted

Efficiency

  • Streamlined
  • Reduced
  • Automated
  • Optimized
  • Cut

Creation

  • Built
  • Launched
  • Designed
  • Developed
  • Created

Analysis

  • Analyzed
  • Evaluated
  • Identified
  • Researched
  • Assessed

Collaboration

  • Partnered
  • Coordinated
  • Facilitated
  • Collaborated
  • Aligned

Step 2 — Add Specific Numbers

Numbers are the most credible thing you can put on a resume. They turn vague claims into concrete evidence. If you can quantify something, quantify it.

  • How many people did you manage? ($5 people, $50 people)
  • What was the budget you controlled?
  • By what percentage did you improve something?
  • How much revenue did you generate or save?
  • How many customers, users, or accounts did you handle?
  • What was the timeline? (cut process time from 3 weeks to 4 days)

If you truly can't find a number, use approximations: "~50 clients," "over $200K," "3x faster." Approximate numbers are still stronger than no numbers.

Step 3 — Show the Impact, Not Just the Task

The "result" part of the formula is where most people give up. They stop at the task and never explain why it mattered. The "so what?" is what separates a good bullet from a great one.

Before — task only
Managed the company's Instagram account and created weekly posts.
After — action + task + result
Grew Instagram following from 2,400 to 18,000 in 8 months by launching a weekly behind-the-scenes series, increasing average post reach by 340%.
Before — task only
Helped with customer onboarding and answered support tickets.
After — action + task + result
Reduced average onboarding time from 14 days to 6 by redesigning the welcome flow and creating a self-serve help center, cutting support tickets by 28%.

How Many Bullets Per Role?

A good rule of thumb:

  • Most recent role: 4–6 bullets
  • Previous roles: 2–4 bullets
  • Roles 5+ years ago: 1–2 bullets, or drop entirely

Focus every bullet on what's most relevant to the job you're applying for. Quality over quantity — 4 strong bullets beat 8 weak ones every time.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Listing job duties instead of achievements. Your job description already exists somewhere. Use this space to prove you were good at it.
  • Writing in present tense for past roles. Past roles should use past tense verbs. Only your current role can use present tense.
  • Using the same verb repeatedly. If every bullet starts with "Managed," you look one-dimensional. Vary your verbs.
  • Making bullets too long. One bullet = one idea. If it runs more than two lines, split it or cut the fat.
  • Forgetting soft-skill context. "Reduced churn by 18%" is good. "Reduced churn by 18% by rebuilding the customer success playbook and coaching a team of 6" is better.

What If I Don't Have Hard Numbers?

Not every role is easily quantifiable. Here's how to handle it:

  • Use scope instead of outcome. "Managed a portfolio of 40+ enterprise clients" shows scale even without a % improvement.
  • Describe the before/after state. "Migrated legacy codebase from jQuery to React, eliminating 12,000 lines of deprecated code."
  • Reference recognition. "Recognized as top performer in Q3 2025 across a team of 22 sales reps."
  • Use frequency. "Delivered weekly presentations to C-suite stakeholders across 3 time zones."
