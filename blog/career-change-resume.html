How to Write a Resume When Switching Careers

How to reframe your experience, highlight transferable skills, and make hiring managers see you as a candidate — not a risk.

July 2026 · 10 min read

Career changers have one specific resume problem: everything on your resume screams "wrong industry." The job title, the company names, the jargon — all of it signals "not what we were looking for."

The good news: your experience isn't the problem. The framing is. Here's how to rewrite your resume so hiring managers see what you actually bring, not just what you've done before.

Step 1 — Audit Your Transferable Skills

Before you write a word, map your existing skills to the new role. Go through the job description and mark every requirement. Then, for each one, ask: "Have I done something that proves I can do this?"

Common transferable skills that travel across industries:

Project managementData analysis Stakeholder communicationBudget ownership Team leadershipProcess improvement Customer relationship managementWriting & content Sales & negotiationProblem-solving

The goal isn't to pretend you have experience you don't. It's to surface the genuine overlap between what you've done and what the new role needs.

Step 2 — Add a Professional Summary

Career changers need a summary at the top of the resume more than anyone else. This is your chance to tell the story before the recruiter starts looking for reasons to say no.

A strong career change summary does three things:

  • Acknowledges the transition (briefly and confidently)
  • Names the most relevant skills you're bringing over
  • States clearly what you're aiming for in this role
Weak summary (no bridge)
"Experienced teacher looking to transition into project management. Fast learner, good communicator."
Strong summary (builds the bridge)
"Former high school department head with 7 years of experience managing curriculum development projects, coordinating cross-functional teams, and tracking outcomes data for 600+ students. Now channeling those same planning and stakeholder management skills into a project management career, with a PMP certification earned in March 2026."

Step 3 — Reframe Your Work Experience

You don't need to delete your old experience — you need to rewrite it through the lens of the new role. Pull out every project, responsibility, or outcome that maps to your target field, and lead with those.

For each past role, ask: What did I do that overlaps with what my target job requires? Write bullets that emphasize those things. Bury or cut bullets that have zero relevance.

Marketing manager applying for a UX researcher role — original bullet
Managed paid ad campaigns across Google and Meta with a $200K/month budget.
Reframed for UX research relevance
Conducted A/B testing and user behavior analysis across 12 ad campaigns, synthesizing findings into actionable creative briefs that improved conversion rates by 22%.

Step 4 — Lead With a Skills Section

For career changers, moving the Skills section near the top (just below the summary) helps recruiters immediately see your relevant capabilities before they dig into a work history that might look confusing.

List only the skills that are directly relevant to the target role. This is not the place to list everything you've ever done. Keep it focused: 8–12 specific, targeted skills.

Step 5 — Fill Gaps With Coursework and Projects

If there's a meaningful skills gap between your background and the new field, show you've done something about it:

  • Certifications — List them with the issuer and date (Google Project Management Certificate, 2026)
  • Online courses — Coursera, LinkedIn Learning, Udemy certificates relevant to the new field
  • Projects — Freelance work, side projects, volunteer roles that gave you hands-on experience in the new area
  • Portfolio — If the new field is creative or technical, link to a GitHub repo, Behance, or portfolio site

Should You Use a Functional Resume Format?

The internet will tell you career changers should use a "functional resume" (skills-based, with work history buried). Don't.

Recruiters and ATS systems hate functional resumes. They look evasive and make it hard to understand your career timeline. Use a reverse-chronological format — just restructure what you emphasize within each role, as described above.

Tailor Every Single Application

Career changers cannot send the same resume to 100 jobs. You're already at a disadvantage compared to people with direct experience — the only way to compensate is to show you've done the work to understand what each specific role needs and that your background maps directly to it.

Read every job description carefully. Adjust your summary, reorder your skills, and swap in the most relevant bullets for each application. It takes longer, but the response rate difference is significant.
